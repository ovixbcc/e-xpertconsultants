Most business owners know their team spends too much time on repetitive work: copying data between systems, chasing approvals, sending the same reminders every week. Automation can take that work away. The hard part is knowing where to begin.

The mistake we see most often is trying to automate everything at once. A large, all-in project takes months, touches every team, and is hard to measure. A better approach is to pick **one process**, automate it well, show the result, and then move to the next.

## Step 1: List the work that repeats

Ask each team lead to write down the tasks they or their team do every day or every week that follow the same steps each time. Typical examples:

- Entering orders from a website or Facebook page into a spreadsheet or accounting system
- Sending payment reminders to customers
- Preparing the same weekly sales or stock report
- Collecting approvals for purchases or leave by email or WhatsApp
- Copying booking details from one system to another

You will usually end up with 15 to 30 items. That list is your automation backlog.

## Step 2: Score each process

For each item, ask four simple questions:

1. **How often does it happen?** Daily work pays back faster than monthly work.
2. **How long does it take each time?** Multiply by frequency to get hours per month.
3. **How costly are mistakes?** Wrong invoices or missed payments matter more than a late internal report.
4. **Are the steps clear?** If people do it differently every time, fix the process before automating it.

The best first candidate is frequent, time-consuming, error-prone and already well defined.

## Step 3: Fix the process before you automate it

Automating a messy process only makes the mess happen faster. Before building anything, map the current steps on one page: who does what, in which system, and where the handoffs are. Remove steps that add nothing, agree on one way of working, and write it down.

> A good rule: if you cannot explain the process to a new employee in ten minutes, it is not ready to automate.

## Step 4: Choose the simplest tool that works

Not every automation needs custom software. Depending on the task, the right tool might be:

- A built-in feature of software you already pay for, such as automatic reminders in your accounting system
- A no-code workflow tool that connects your apps
- A small custom script or integration when the systems do not talk to each other
- A full ERP or CRM when many processes depend on the same data

Start with what you already have. Add new tools only when they solve a clear problem.

## Step 5: Measure before and after

Record a baseline before you change anything: how many hours the task takes, how many errors happen, how long customers wait. After the automation has run for a month, measure again. Real numbers make it easy to decide what to automate next, and to get the team on board.

## Step 6: Train the team and keep improving

People need to trust the new way of working. Show them what changed, what they no longer need to do, and who to ask when something looks wrong. Then review the process every few months. Business needs change, and good automation changes with them.
