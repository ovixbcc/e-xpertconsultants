Tourning On started with a simple observation: tourists in Bangladesh want more than a hotel room and a beach. They want to *do* things, such as ride, explore, rent and join activities, and they want to arrange it themselves. The idea was an **activity-based tourism platform**, starting as a self-driven rental service, and it went from idea to reality in **six months**.

## The traction was real

During the trial period in Cox's Bazar, the platform drew strong interest from tourists. People liked being able to find and book activities and rentals on their own terms instead of negotiating with whoever happened to be standing on the beach. On the demand side, the idea worked.

## Why it still had to close in Cox's Bazar

Demand alone did not make the business viable. Three problems came together:

- **A vulnerable business ground.** Tourism in a single destination depends on season, weather and local conditions that a young company cannot control.
- **Local threats.** A new, transparent way of doing business met resistance on the ground, which made day-to-day operations harder and riskier.
- **Complex operations.** Rentals mean physical assets, maintenance, insurance, damage handling, deposits and on-ground staff. Every booking carried operational risk that software alone could not remove.

The result was a hard decision: shut down operations in Cox's Bazar, where the journey began.

## Lessons for anyone building in tourism

**1. Validate the ground, not just the demand.** Tourists wanting a service is only half the test. The other half is whether the local environment, including suppliers, authorities and existing operators, will let you deliver it safely and profitably.

**2. Separate the platform from the assets.** Owning or directly managing rental assets adds cost and risk. A marketplace model, where verified local partners provide the activities and the platform handles discovery, booking and payment, scales with far less exposure.

**3. Bring local partners in early.** Instead of competing with local operators, give them a reason to join: more bookings, digital payments and fair, transparent pricing. Partners who earn from the platform defend it rather than fight it.

**4. Secure payments and deposits.** Damage, no-shows and disputes are where tourism businesses lose money and trust. Holding payments and deposits in escrow until the activity is completed protects both the tourist and the provider.

**5. Do not depend on one destination.** Launch in one place to learn, but design the model so it can move to other destinations quickly when local conditions turn against you.
